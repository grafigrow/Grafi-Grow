How I Illustrated a Children's Book in 2 Days — AI-Assisted, Human-Directed

A children's picture book lives or dies on consistency. A child must recognise the same girl, the same grandmother, the same tree on page 1 and page 24. That is the hard part — not drawing one pretty picture, but drawing twenty-four that belong together.

I recently illustrated a full picture book, The Great Tree — A Story in 21 Seeds: 24 illustrations in two days, cover included. The story follows a giant ancient tree losing its grey leaves, a grandmother, and a group of children who help it turn green again, one small lesson at a time.

Here is the workflow I used. It is AI-assisted, human-directed — AI generated the images, I directed every character, every colour, and every fix.

1. Character sheets first, scenes second

I did not start with the scenes. I started with the people.

For each main character I made a reference sheet: front view and three-quarter view, plus close-ups for happy, worried, and determined expressions, on a plain white background. Mira, the 8-year-old main girl, in a yellow t-shirt and rolled-up blue jeans. Teo, 9, red curly hair and a red hoodie. Grandmother Ama, very old, deep red headscarf and a rust-and-maroon paisley shawl.

Why first? Because once a character sheet is approved, every later picture has something to match. Without it, a character's hair, clothes, or age drifts from page to page — and children notice immediately.

My rule: never use a character's name alone in a prompt. I paste the full description, word for word, every time. "Wavy" never becomes "curly" halfway through a book.

2. One style anchor for the whole book

Next I made one style anchor: the Great Tree itself, with no people — gnarled trunk, wide branches, a small village, rolling hills, and a kingfisher on a low branch. Soft watercolour with fine ink lines, warm natural light, gentle storybook mood, no text in any image.

That one picture became the style reference for everything else. Same light, same line, same mood. If a new scene started to look too bright, too realistic, or too pen-and-ink, I went back to the anchor instead of accepting it.

I also set a colour timeline for the tree, and wrote it down before I started: grey leaves falling at the start, only a few small green leaves; a broken branch after the storm; green returning slowly on the stream side first; mostly green near the end; fully green for the final pages. A child can follow the story just by watching the tree change.

3. Easiest scenes first, hardest last

I worked in story order, but I ordered the difficulty: easy scenes first to build confidence, hardest last. A quiet scene with one child came before a festival scene with six people. The cover — eight people, hardest of all — came last.

For each scene I generated several versions, picked the best, and fixed only what was wrong. One wrong child in a good picture? Fix that child only. Do not throw the whole picture away and start again — you will lose the good parts with it.

Then, before moving on, I checked every picture against a short list: hair, clothes, glasses, skin tone, height, and the tree colour for that point in the story. Nina is always the smallest. Jonah is always the biggest. That check takes minutes and saves hours of rework.

4. What "2 days" really meant

Two days was tight, and I will be honest about that. It worked because the plan was ready before the first image: cast descriptions, style anchor, tree timeline, file names in story order, and a clear delivery list.

Day one was anchor, character sheets, and the first easy scenes. Day two was the remaining scenes, fixes, the cover, and delivery: final upscaled images, named in story order, print-ready at 300 DPI for Amazon KDP, plus the character sheets so the author could make cast line-ups.

Speed came from the system, not from skipping quality. Every final image was checked against the cast table and the tree timeline before delivery.

5. What AI does — and what it does not do

AI made the images fast. It did not decide the story, the characters, or the consistency. I did that, page by page.

That is why I describe this work as AI-assisted, not hand-drawn watercolour. If you hire an illustrator who works this way, ask them how they keep characters consistent, how they handle fixes, and what you get at delivery. The answers matter more than any single pretty sample.
