How to Brief an Illustrator for Your KDP Picture Book

A good brief saves you money, time, and rework. A vague brief does the opposite — the illustrator guesses, you ask for changes, and the book drifts.

I illustrate picture books for Amazon KDP and IngramSpark, AI-assisted and human-directed. The books that go smoothly all start the same way: the author sends me a clear, short brief before I draw anything.

Here is what to send, in order.

1. Your manuscript, finished

Send the full text, not a summary. Page by page, if you can — one spread per page, with a note for what should be in the picture.

Example:

Page 4-5: Mira finds the pouch under the tree. She is alone. The tree has grey leaves falling. Evening light.

That one line tells me the character, the place, the mood, and the tree colour. A summary like "a girl finds something" tells me none of it.

If your text is not final, say so — I can start with a character sheet while you finish it.

2. Your trim size and page count

This decides the shape of every image. Tell me:

  • Trim size: for example, 8.5 x 8.5 inches, square. That is the most common KDP picture-book size, but tell me yours — do not assume I know it.
  • Page count: for example, 24 pages, 32 pages. KDP paperbacks have a minimum page count, and the spine and bleed change with the size.
  • Format: paperback, hardcover, or eBook? A cover for paperback and a cover for hardcover are not the same file.

If you do not know your trim size yet, pick it before we start. Changing it halfway through means resizing or redrawing images, and that costs time.

3. Your characters, described once, in full

Write each main character down once, completely, and do not change it later. Use the same words every time.

A good character description looks like this:

Mira, 8, light tan skin, long wavy light-brown hair, brown eyes, yellow t-shirt, rolled-up blue jeans, white sneakers.

Add age, height, and anything that must never change — glasses, a headscarf, a favourite toy. If two children share a main colour, change one of them: in a busy scene, colour is how a child finds the right character.

4. Your style, in three pictures

Do not send twenty style references. Send three:

  1. One picture that is closest to the look you want — soft watercolour, bold digital, pastel, or something else.
  2. One picture for the mood — warm and gentle, funny, or adventurous.
  3. One picture for what you do not want — too realistic, too dark, too busy.

Tell me in one sentence what you like about each one. "I like the warm light in this one" is more useful than "I like this style."

My own style is soft watercolour with fine ink lines, warm natural light, and rounded childlike faces. If that is what you want, say so — and if you want something different, say that too, before I start.

5. Your story timeline, if anything changes

Does a tree change colour? Does a season change? Does a character grow up, get a haircut, or lose a tooth? Write it down as a short timeline.

In The Great Tree, the tree went from grey to green across the story, and I tracked it page by page. Without that timeline, the pictures would have contradicted the story. If nothing changes, say "nothing changes."

6. Timeline, budget, and files

Tell me three practical things up front:

  • Deadline: when do you need the finished book? A short book usually takes 7 to 10 days after the sample spread is approved. A full book takes longer. A rush deadline is possible, but say so at the start.
  • Budget: what is your range? My starter price is $100 for 20 images — see my pricing post for what that covers. A full 32-page book costs more, and I will quote it before I start.
  • Files you need: print-ready images at 300 DPI for KDP, plus character sheets? A cover with bleed? An eBook version too? Tell me, so I deliver the right files the first time.

7. What happens next

Here is the process I use, so you know what to expect:

  1. Character sheet first. I draw your main characters. You approve faces, clothes, and colours.
  2. One sample spread. I illustrate one spread from your manuscript. You approve the style on your own story.
  3. The full book, in story order. Easiest scenes first, hardest last, cover last.
  4. Checks and delivery. Every image is checked against the character list and the story timeline, then delivered named in story order, print-ready.

You get fixes for anything that does not match the brief. A complete change of style after the sample is approved is a new quote, not a fix.

Copy-paste brief checklist

Send me this, filled in:

  • Full manuscript, page by page
  • Trim size, page count, paperback / hardcover / eBook
  • Character descriptions, word for word, for each main character
  • 3 style references, with one line each on what you like
  • Story timeline, if anything changes
  • Deadline and budget
  • Files you need at delivery

If you can fill in most of that, you are ready. If you cannot fill in the characters or the trim size yet, start there — those two answers unlock everything else.
